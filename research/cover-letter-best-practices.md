# Cover letter — practical guide (English only)

**Verification legend** (same convention as `resume-best-practices.md`)
- `FACT` — verifiable, documented, cited in the source report.
- `OPINION` — recommendation or practitioner consensus, not independently measured.
- `synthesis` — this project's own inference, not a claim from any source.

> Full evidence base with every citation: `research/cover-letter-full-report.md`
> (or the hosted version, linked in Sources below). This file only carries what
> actually changes how a letter gets written.

## TL;DR

1. **Write one for every serious application**, even where the posting marks it optional.
   `synthesis` — tech is the industry where skipping is most sanctioned, but a senior,
   international-remote, low-volume relationship-driven hiring profile
   is exactly the profile where letters get read closely, not skimmed. Adjust for your own target profile.
2. **200–300 words, one page, three paragraphs** (open / body / close). Don't restate the
   résumé — connect one or two achievements to what the posting actually asks for.
3. **Personalization is non-negotiable.** The single most cross-corroborated finding in
   the whole research: name the company, the role, and one detail a competitor's letter
   couldn't also use.
4. **One generic base, English only.** Country-specific formatting doesn't apply — every
   target market here reads letters the same transactional way, and Brazilian companies
   don't ask for one at all. The lever that matters is **role**, not geography: a web2
   posting and a web3 posting call for a different angle; a US posting and a German one
   don't.
5. **AI-authenticity rule carries over unchanged from the résumés.** `FACT`, independently
   corroborated by three 2024–2025 surveys: AI-assisted drafting isn't the penalty —
   genericness and cross-applicant sameness are. Co-write, then run through `tropes.md`,
   same as every résumé bullet.

## 1. Why write one at all

- Cover letters are optional at roughly half of large tech employers, and only ~2% of
  tech listings on Indeed explicitly require one. `FACT`
- The calculus flips for this specific profile: screeners on high-volume/entry postings
  skip fastest, but final decision-makers on low-volume, senior, or relationship-driven
  roles weight a letter as a real tie-breaker. Web3/DeFi hiring in particular runs through
  smaller, high-context teams and referrals rather than high-volume ATS funnels — the same
  dynamic already established for résumés in `resume-best-practices.md` §7. `synthesis`,
  built on a sourced finding.
- Practical rule: always write one, but keep it proportionate. A rushed, generic letter is
  worse than none — "generic/templated" is the single most cross-corroborated rejection
  cause in the source research. `FACT`

## 2. Length & structure

- **200–300 words** — the low end of the general 250–400 convergence. If your résumé
  already leans dense and senior-confident (one-page discipline), the letter should match
  that same signal-density instinct rather than sprawl. `synthesis`, applying a
  `FACT`-tier finding.
- Three-part structure:
  1. **Open (1–2 sentences):** name the role + one concrete, specific detail about the
     company — a shipped feature, a protocol they build on, something from their public
     work. Never a generic "I am writing to apply for..." — the most-flagged outdated
     opener across every source consulted.
  2. **Body (1–2 short paragraphs):** one or two achievements from
     `profile/experience-bank.md`, chosen and phrased to answer what *this posting* asks
     for — not a tour of the résumé.
  3. **Close (1–2 sentences):** reaffirm interest specifically, ask for a concrete next
     step. Not "looking forward to hearing from you."

## 3. What actually varies: role, not geography

This is the one place the general research pointed one way and this project is choosing
another, deliberately: the source report spends real depth on country-by-country format
rules (Germany's rigid *Anschreiben*, France's *lettre de motivation*, etc.). None of that
applies here if every market you're targeting reads an English cover letter the same
transactional way, and Brazilian postings don't request one at all. So the customization
axis for this project is **role type**, not location:

- **Web3/DeFi postings:** lean on proof-of-work — name the protocol/project directly where
  the work is already public and credited, point at verifiable artifacts (GitHub, public
  announcement threads) rather than asserting reliability in the abstract. Where a client
  relationship isn't publicly named, stay generic ("a multichain lending protocol") per
  your own client-naming policy in `profile/experience-bank.md`.
- **Web2 / general backend-full-stack postings:** stays closer to the classic
  transactional convention — direct, metrics-forward, no crypto-specific framing. Your
  highest-SLA signature API work is the anchor achievement.
- **The generic base** (used when there's no time to tailor) needs to work for either —
  see §5.

## 4. AI-authenticity — unchanged from the résumés

- The single best-triangulated finding in the whole research: AI-assisted drafting isn't
  the penalty; genericness and cross-applicant sameness are. `FACT`, independently
  corroborated by ResumeBuilder (2024), TopResume (2025), and Forbes reporting (Oct 2025)
  — three unrelated sources converging on the same distinction.
- Same discipline as résumé bullets: co-write, then filter through `tropes.md`. Cover
  letters are more narrative than résumé bullets, which is exactly where these tropes
  creep in — watch in particular for negative parallelism ("It's not X, it's Y") in the
  opening hook, "Here's the thing about..." false-suspense opens, and bold-first bullets
  if the body ever breaks into a list.

## 5. The generic base — what it needs to do

For applications where tailoring isn't worth the time, one English letter needs to stand
on its own without a specific company/role plugged in beyond `[Company]` / `[Role]`
placeholders. To do that it should:

- Lead with an identity, not a company-specific hook (the specific hook is what per-role
  tailoring adds back in) — something like "product-minded backend engineer who owns
  systems end-to-end," per the positioning already drafted in `profile/narrative.md`.
- Anchor on the single strongest, most broadly legible achievement rather than a
  web2-only or web3-only one — a public, verifiable, quantified open-source contribution
  (e.g. a measurable performance win shipped as a breaking major release) reads as strong
  signal regardless of whether the target role is web2 or web3.
- Still pass every check in §6 below — genericness in the *filler* is fine to avoid by
  being well-written; genericness in *content* (no company specifics) is the one thing
  the base is explicitly allowed to have, because that's what tailoring exists to fix.

## 6. Common mistakes to guard against

- Restating résumé bullets verbatim instead of connecting one or two to the role — the
  same failure mode already flagged for résumés, doubly costly here since the letter's
  entire value is the connective tissue the résumé can't provide.
- Generic opening line — avoid on the same grounds the résumé avoids an objective
  statement.
- Skipping personalization to save time under application volume — contradicts the
  strongest finding in the whole research. Better to send fewer, well-targeted
  applications with real letters than many generic ones — which is exactly why the
  generic base exists as a deliberate, separate tier, not a shortcut disguised as
  tailoring.

## 7. Open questions / follow-ups

1. **File location:** `cover-letters/generic-en.md` for the base, mirroring
   `resumes/<pivot>/<lang>.md`; per-role tailors live under
   `applications/<company-role>/cover-letter.md`, copied from the base using the same
   two-commit base→tailor workflow already used for résumés.
2. Once the generic base is written and settled, decide whether it's worth splitting into
   web2/web3 pivot-specific bases (§3) or whether tailoring the generic one per
   application is fast enough to skip that step.
